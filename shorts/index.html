The Crossing

The boat holds about a hundred of us. I try counting heads and give up somewhere in the seventies because the woman next to me starts talking.

Her name is Ines. She has a scar behind her right ear, pale and straight, about an inch long. There is a word for people with that scar and it is not a kind one, and I am ashamed to say it is the first thing that arrives in my head.

She catches me looking.

“Go ahead,” she says. “Ask.”

“Let me guess. Focus? Memory? I hear the new firmware lets you stream Instagram while you brush your teeth.”

“Seizures,” she says. “Four a day since I was nineteen. Shearwater’s implant ended them in an afternoon. I got three hours of my life back every day.”

She says it without any edge at all, which is worse. She goes back to watching the island grow on the horizon, and I stand there holding my drink, and the word “tagged” sits in my mouth like a shard of glass I would very much like to spit out.

She forgives me by the time the dock is in view. She asks what I do, and I give the answer that makes people’s eyes glaze over at parties: consulting, between things, you know how it is. She nods but seems distracted. Then she asks to see my invitation.

It is a heavy grey card with my name on it in real ink. Embossed above the name in gold: a seabird banking low over a line of waves, one wingtip touching the water. There is no company name anywhere on the card. She holds it up to read it against the light, and the wind takes it out of her fingers. It flips twice and settles on the water, gold bird up, riding the swell.

She apologizes. For a second I think she is going to cry, which is a strange amount of feeling to spend on someone else’s stationery.

“They’ll have you on a list,” she says. “Nobody gets turned away.”

I will spend a lot of the rest of my life thinking about these words.

Around us, ninety-some people in beautiful clothes are all being casual at each other. Nobody knows the dress code. A silver-haired man takes his jacket off, watches the upper deck for a minute, and puts it back on. When a tray of small dark things on crackers comes around, everyone declines until one man takes one, and then everybody indulges.

The island belongs to the man who owns Shearwater, or the island is called Shearwater and the company is named after it. Nobody on the boat seems sure, and it does not seem to matter. That is what that kind of money does. It turns humans into gods and truth into legend.

The Door

The dock is lit with paper lanterns and staffed by young people in grey linen who all look like they sleep nine hours a night. At the end of the dock there are white tables, and on the tables there are neat stacks of paper. Liability waivers. Eleven pages, the gold bird at the top of every one. My stomach tightens before I can put my finger on why.

A man ahead of me in line, silver hair and a signet ring, flips through his copy and laughs.

“This wouldn’t survive five minutes in front of a judge,” he says.

The woman with him does not laugh. “Look at a map before you get too confident,” she says quietly. “The island sits in a seam between Tongan and Fijian water, claimed by both, governed by neither. Places like this don’t get bought for the view.”

He signs anyway. Everybody signs. Nobody reads anything they are already planning to survive.

I am halfway through my signature when an elbow catches mine and a glass of wine goes across the page. Ines, appearing from nowhere, apologizing again, dabbing at the ruined paper with a napkin and making it worse. A staff member brings me a fresh copy with a fresh smile. It costs me two minutes, and I do not read the second copy either.

At the end of the tables they fasten a bracelet on each of us, woven black cord with a small gold amulet stamped with the bird, heavy for its size, and somehow warm to the touch. The clasp closes against my wrist bone with a click.

Our phones stay with us, which surprises me at first. I then realize there’s no signal on the island except one app, already installed somehow, sitting on my home screen with a gold icon and no name. The bird again. I open my camera app and get a black screen with small white text: Unauthorized zone for this app.

The Clock

The lawn is the size of a runway and the house above it is lit like a cruise ship. I drift and eavesdrop, which is what you do at a party where you know no one.

By the fountain, two men are laughing. “Twice,” one of them says. “He turned them down twice, and now here he is drinking their wine.”

A woman in green is explaining herself to a circle of nodding heads. “It is a patent, not a blockade. They can license it like everyone else.” Her glass has been empty the whole time and nobody has offered to fix it.

On the terrace steps a tall man keeps saying “thresholds.” “The bill sets thresholds. It does not name any company.” He says it the way you say a thing you have already said in front of a committee.

And a woman stands alone at the terrace railing, familiar in a way that bothers me for an hour before it surfaces: a byline. A story about implant telemetry that ran for one morning two years ago and then was never mentioned again by anyone, including her paper, including her.

It takes me three drinks to see the shape of it. Everyone at this party has, at some point, told Shearwater no.

Except me. Nobody has ever asked me for anything, including whoever wrote the guest list. Three separate guests ask what I do, and three separate gazes drift over my shoulder halfway through my answer. I decide the invitation came out of an algorithm and that I am its exploration budget, the random sample a model spends to find out what nobodies do at parties like this. Every model keeps a few of us on the books, or its picture of the world goes stale.

A big man named Walsh adopts me for a while near the bar. Private equity, gold watch, the kind of warmth that expects a return. He keeps glasses full. Not just mine. He moves through the crowd topping people up, ordering rounds of things with ages in their names, steering the nervous ones back into conversation with a hand on the shoulder. He is the most generous man at the party and I never once see him finish a drink of his own.

At nine the host appears on the terrace, and the lawn goes quiet the way a theater does when the lights go out.

Keller. He is slim and grey and pleasant, and he looks like the photograph of himself. He stands with his hands loose at his sides, which almost nobody can do.

“Welcome to Shearwater,” he says. “Every one of you was chosen carefully. We have quite a cross section tonight: founders, builders, journalists, one or two of the people who write the rules the rest of us live under. I know Shearwater is not universally loved on this lawn. I hope that by morning we will have won some of you over.”

Nobody laughs. The crowd shifts, a hundred people deciding at the same moment to keep their faces pleasant.

At his signal, staff pull a black canvas away, and in the middle of the lawn there is a clock. It is enormous, brass and glass, on a plinth like a monument, and its hands stand at zero. Around the face, where the hour numbers should be, there are engravings in a rising spiral. It takes me a moment to see that they are dollar amounts, and another moment to see how they end. The last engraving before the spiral runs off the face is not a number. It is the bird, small and tidy, wingtip touching a wave.

“A game,” says Keller. “Tap your phone to your bracelet, please.”

A hundred people obey him at once. The app opens showing an account. My name, and a balance of zero. Under it, a chart projecting the night forward. The line starts shallow and then bends upward until the top of the screen cuts it off, my name printed over it.

“Every hour, on the hour, the clock pays,” Keller says. “The amounts double as you see. Leave whenever you like. Boats depart on the hour, every hour, and whatever your balance shows when you step aboard is yours, wired before you reach the mainland. There is only one rule.” He smiles like a man reading the smile off a card. “The last five guests at this party become the property of Shearwater. It is all in the waiver. Page nine, I believe.”

Silence, then laughter at the obvious joke.

“If any of you find this distasteful, the first boat leaves in ten minutes. No balance, no hard feelings. I am so pleased you came.”

A murmur goes across the crowd. About a dozen people take the first boat out. Watching them go, I feel the first genuine emotion of the evening and it embarrasses me: I feel superior to them. Ninety-odd guests, five losers, and you only lose if you stay to the bitter end of a party. Those are wonderful odds. The dock empties and the rest of us stand a little taller, having sorted ourselves into the brave and the naive.

Keller waits until the boat is a light on the water. Then he says, as if remembering a small domestic detail:

“Oh. One more thing. Not everyone here tonight is a guest. Some of the people around you are with the company. Actors, if you like. They will eat with you and drink with you and they will not leave, and they do not count toward the final five.” He lets that settle to the bottom. “I mention it only so that no one relies on arithmetic. Arithmetic would spoil the mood.”

Nobody says anything for three full seconds, and then everyone says everything at once. Underneath all of it the crowd is already moving. Sixty people drifting toward the dock, each moving just slowly enough to deny it later.

That is when the clock strikes its first hour, one soft bell across the lawn, and every phone chimes at once.

I look down. Fifty thousand dollars. My name over it.

Around me the drift toward the dock slows like a wave on a beach. People stop to look at their screens. I watch a hundred faces do the same private math in the same half second, and the math says: the boats run every hour. There will always be another boat.

People hate making decisions. Keller never makes us decide anything. Every hour he simply lets us fail to.

Hour One

Thirty people take the one o’clock boat, or whatever the hour is really called. The clock has its own time now and the lawn has stopped using any other. The thirty leave with fifty thousand dollars each and the particular look of people telling themselves they are the smart ones. The rest of us congratulate ourselves for staying.

A fund manager named Levin sets up shop by the clock and does everyone’s thinking out loud, free of charge.

“Ninety guests minus plants,” Levin says, “call the plants ten percent, that’s low eighties, minus the thirty that just left. Say fifty of us are real. Five lose. Ten percent chance of ruin at the tail, but you’re not staying to the tail, are you. You’re staying to hour eight, nine at most. Hour nine pays twelve million eight hundred thousand.” He taps his phone against the amulet like a man knocking on wood. “The trick is simple. If we all just stay a bit longer than we normally would have, we’ll all be that much richer for it.”

Walsh, meanwhile, has bought the whole terrace a round of something older than most marriages. A woman near the dock, coat already on, lets him talk her back up the lawn with a hand on her shoulder and a joke I cannot hear. Her coat comes off again.

Hour Three

Two hundred thousand dollars arrives in my account with another chime. It is more than I made in some years.

The Reeds argue by the boathouse, in the hushed way married people argue in public, mouths barely moving. He is holding both their coats. She is not looking at the dock. When the bell sounds he boards alone and stands at the stern, and she stays on the lawn with her arms crossed. For the rest of the night I watch her check her phone despite the lack of service.

Ines finds me twice this hour, both times just before the bell, both times with someone I absolutely must meet, and both times the someone is standing within ten steps of the gangway. A politician. A conservationist. I keep drifting back up the lawn to the clock and she keeps not quite saying anything, smiling her boat smile. On the boat this morning I could not get her to stop talking. Tonight the introductions are all there is.

Just then the pieces come together.

The actors must be employees, and like all Shearwater employees, they get the implant when they join the company. Which means the actors are tagged and the implant scars are my way to win this game. I stand by the clock turning slowly with my drink like a lighthouse, counting scars behind ears. I count eleven and breathe a sigh of relief. Eleven plants. The rest of us are real. The simple arithmetic Keller warned us against is right there behind everyone’s right ear if you only know where to look.

Hour Five

Eight hundred thousand dollars. The first plant is caught.

A waiter drops a tray by the fountain. The whole lawn flinches, a dozen conversations snapping off mid-word. All but one. The man by the hedge is halfway through a story about a sailing trip and he talks straight through the crash without missing a beat. His voice holding the same friendly pitch while every other voice on the lawn has stopped. For three long seconds his is the only sound at the party, something pleasant about the wind off Sardinia. Everyone hears it.

A crowd gathers. People ask him questions with edges on them and he answers every one, warmly, at length, without ever quite saying anything. He agrees the party is strange. He agrees the game is cruel. He agrees with whatever is put in front of him and keeps talking. It is like arguing with a recording. The crowd runs out of words before he does. He is still mid-anecdote when they start backing away.

Forty people take the next boat. It rides low in the water.

The lawn afterward feels like a theater after the fire alarm, half empty, the party continuing out of muscle memory. Levin leaves at hour six, the hour he always said he would, with one million six hundred thousand dollars, straightening his jacket like a man stepping out of a casino after a good night. Walsh boards behind him with a bottle under each arm and tips an invisible hat to the herd he has spent all night fattening. Some of them wave back.

The rest of us count each other. That is all anyone does now. The visible crowd is maybe thirty, and the question no one says out loud stands on the lawn with us: thirty minus how many actors. Nobody seems to know except me.

Hour Seven

Three million two hundred thousand dollars.

The eleven I counted are all still on the lawn. I have watched them not leave all night: not at the exodus, not after the tray, not once. Their demeanor never changes.

So I spend the hour carefully recruiting. A quiet word by the bar. Another at the buffet. Ears checked by pretending to fix a collar, and only the scarless invited.

We meet by the boathouse, away from the clock’s light, eight of us. Mrs. Reed, with her phone still in her hand. The tall man from the terrace steps, the staffer who drafts bills for senators. The journalist. The quiet CEO of a microchip company. Three scarless others whose faces I trusted, and me. All verified.

“Five is the trap,” I tell them, keeping my voice under the sound of the water. “When the bell sounds all eight of us walk together, straight aboard. No speeches, no goodbyes, no last look at the chart. Eight people leaving at once cannot be the last five people at a party.”

“And if somebody in this boathouse is lying?” Mrs. Reed asks, checking each of our ears one more time.

“Then we are dead already,” the journalist says, and laughs.

The bell sounds. We walk. I am fourth in the line and my whole body is already at the water, already home, already telling this story at dinners for the rest of my life.

At the gangway, three of them abruptly stop walking.

The CEO and two others stop at the same instant without a word between them. They stand at the foot of the gangway smiling pleasantly at nothing, hands loose at their sides.

“What’s wrong?” I ask, even though I already know. “Why did you stop?”

On the gangway the staffer is clutching the rail with both hands, the journalist is shouting something about all of us going together, and Mrs. Reed is looking back up the lawn at the clock in a panic. The five of us jump aboard, and this time the boat does not move. No horn, no engine, no crew. From the deck I watch a procession of lanterns come down the lawn toward the dock.

Ines is standing with the rest of the actors. Lantern light, pleasant face, eyes on me. Down at her side her hand twitches.

Every phone on the island chimes at once.

THE GAME HAS CONCLUDED. THANK YOU FOR STAYING.

Mrs. Reed breaks into a sob while the staffer turns pale.

The House

Below the house it is white and quiet. There is no clock anywhere.

They are kind to us. Nobody is dragged. There are couches. A young man in grey linen explains, with real warmth, that the other four are going home tonight. There is just one stop first, a room at the end of the corridor, twenty minutes each. The door of the room is white, and the bird is on it at eye level, gold on white, wingtip touching a wave. Mrs. Reed will go home and sign the licensing agreement the Reeds have refused for three years. The staffer will make one small edit to one clause of one bill. The journalist will decide that the story was thin. They will remember a wonderful party. Their balances, he assures us, will be paid in full.

Mrs. Reed goes first. She comes back twenty minutes later with a familiar smile, thanking the technician for a lovely evening, wondering whether her husband will still be up when she gets in. The staffer goes next, then the journalist, then the quiet man from the boathouse.

“And me,” I say. “What do you want me for?”

Keller answers. I had not seen him come in. Up close he is smaller. Now that I can see his eyes, I notice that they somehow do not match his face.

“Nothing,” Keller says. “The others are acquisitions. You are a blank canvas.”

“Hosts wear out,” he says. “This body was somebody else’s eleven years ago. It is due. And the position has requirements the other four could never meet. You are unremarkable. Unattached. Unknown. A face people forget while they are still looking at it.” He studies me with something that is almost kindness. “We do extensive background checks for every guest. Nobody is waiting up for you.”

The technician who preps me is gentle and chatty. The old units, she says, anchored at the mastoid, behind the ear. Left a mark, no way around it. The new generation goes in through the sinus. Twenty minutes. No mark at all.

I want to tell you it went dark. A light switched off, a name erased. It is not what they built. The truth is that nothing goes anywhere. I am all still here. Every part of me that laughs at the wrong moments and lies about consulting and still knows my dead mother’s phone number by heart is present, seated at the window of the locked room.

It is only that the hands are driven from elsewhere now. I watch as my hand rises in front of my face. My fingers flex: one two three four five, like a man trying on gloves.

After

There are ninety-some of them on the boat and nobody knows the dress code.

I watch it come in from the end of the dock, lanterns lit, waivers stacked on the white tables, a large black canvas on the lawn. They gave me a name on the drive down to the water. It is a good name. It sounds like old money. There is a gold bird on my cufflinks now, wingtip touching a wave. Out past the dock, real shearwaters are working the swell in the last of the light, flying so low that their wingtips cut the water and leave almost no trace. There are photographs of me at charity dinners I never attended, in a life I have owned for six weeks. When the guests search the name on the boat ride over, the face that comes up will be this one, smiling the way I now smile, which is very well.

The boat docks. They come down the gangway being casual at each other, jackets coming off and going back on, nobody the first to touch the trays, all of them quietly astonished that a man like me knows their names. I know all their names.

Ines disembarks from the boat in grey linen, radiant, her scar a thin pale line in the lantern light. Our eyes meet as I welcome her onto the island. There is a window in hers too. There has been all along. Someone is at it, screaming.

Her hand twitches softly at her side.

And low against my trouser seam, where nobody is looking, where even the thing that drives my hands does not bother to look because what could one finger matter, my smallest finger twitches. It is everything I have. Go, she says, and once, just once, I answer her: I know. I know. There is nowhere. But thank you.

The party takes its time, the way ours did. Waivers, bracelets, the lawn filling with beautiful clothes, the clock waiting under its canvas. My body greets and pours and remembers names for two hours. I watch the water darken past the dock.

At nine I walk out onto the terrace, and the lawn goes quiet the way a theater does when the lights go out. My hands hang loose at my sides.

My mouth opens, warm and easy, with the lights of the house behind me, and I hear myself say it in a voice I used to spend carelessly, back when it was mine:

“Welcome to Shearwater. Every one of you was chosen carefully.”
