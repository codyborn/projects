At Crystal Park, up in the Pioneer Mountains, the pick axe caved through the damp soil and roots, revealing a vein of peanut-butter-colored clay. I carefully scraped layer after layer until a perfectly clear crystal popped out and fell to the bottom of the pit. I smeared the clay off and held it up to the light. It was amazing that something residing in the earth for millions of years could emerge so smooth and perfectly clear. My dad pulled his own crystals out of the earth in a nearby hole. As it started getting dark, we hiked down the hill until we found a hand pumped water faucet which we used to clean our findings. Some of the crystals were perfectly clear, others had a dark smokey tint, and a rare few even looked purple.

A couple days later, at the Spokane Bar Sapphire Mine outside Helena, we sifted through wet stones for sapphires. By quickly shaking the rock covered screen back and forth, the dense sapphires collect in the center of the heap. You then carefully flip the pile onto the table, remove the screen and if you're lucky, sitting in the center of the pile will be a clear blue Montana sapphire. The bags of mineral rich dirt are around $100 and are run through a hopper to remove the low density material. My dad and I both found sapphires large enough to cut, worth over $1,000 each. The entire experience felt like a wholesome form of gambling.

Living in Montana reawakened my love for rocks. As a kid, I'd spend hours at the shores of the Colorado River, hunched over hunting for little treasures. One of the common threads in my life (aside from collecting rocks, apparently) has been a love for adventure, and in February I had an idea to go on a real treasure hunt.

I began researching which treasures both had a reasonable probability of discovery and involved some actual adventure. Combing the beaches with a metal detector was not my definition of exciting. After telling my brother about this idea, he pointed me to a Netflix documentary called Gold & Greed. It features a former treasure hunter who hid a chest of treasure somewhere in the American West. This hider, Justin Posey, left a riddle and, in 2025, a book with details on where to find it. My love for both riddles and adventure sealed my fate.

Over the month that I lived in Montana, I drove 8,926 miles, enough to cross the country three times. I bounced around mountainsides with my bear mace getting caught in too many thunderstorms to count. I scoured historic maps and learned about the cultural history of the land, reading books about the American Indians and the diaries of Lewis and Clark. I dug into the local geology and waterways, and learned of the rhythmites: layered beds left by a series of prehistoric glacial floods that ran from Missoula down to the Pacific.

I stayed in a little cabin on the edge of Pony, a hundred-person town where I chopped my own wood for heat. Almost every day, I'd come across a new type of animal that I had never seen before: a den of fox cubs, a herd of pronghorn and one very friendly turkey. The land in Montana is incredibly beautiful and still incredibly wild. Even if the treasure weren't real, I wouldn't be mad for all the wild places it took me and things I got to see. Alas, I left Montana without the treasure but carry with me a new appreciation for some of the wild beauty of my home country.
