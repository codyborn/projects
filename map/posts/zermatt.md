In the shadow of the Matterhorn sits the Swiss town of Zermatt, resting at 5,315 feet. My AI brought me here for more pre-Nepal training and my last chance for acclimatization before the real thing. Cozy streets lined with wooden Alpine buildings offer hot chocolate, sourdough croissants and every type of mountain gear you could imagine.

The surrounding glacier-covered mountains are in perpetual winter. Trains and gondolas carry you to jaw-dropping heights: the cog railway to 10,135 feet, the cable car across the valley to 12,740, the highest cable car station in Europe. The year-round snow attracts skiers and snowboarders, so the gondola queue is half ski boots and half hiking boots.

As part of my acclimatization, I spent the night at the highest hotel in the Swiss Alps: Gornergrat Kulmhotel. As the train climbs, the foliage changes from green to yellow and eventually barren hills of white. Stepping out on the icy train platform, freezing wind whips flurries of snow in your face. The hotel is striking. Built in 1910, it resembles a castle settled on the edge of a cliff. On its two towers rest steel domes, the eyes of the observatory. I unknowingly timed my visit with the one week a year they run a tour of the lab with their resident astronomer. We picked up some stellar trivia from the talk. One that struck me: if you imagine a spiral galaxy to be a spinning octopus, our solar system resides in one arm of that octopus. The Milky Way we see is the rest of that octopus, edge-on from inside our own arm.

After having my mind blown a couple times, I sat down for a four-course dinner while watching the sunset over the Matterhorn. With the sun set, the nearly full moon lit up the whole range, making me wish I had a real camera to capture the scene. The mountains blocking out light pollution from the valley created the perfect setting for pondering one's place in the universe.

As a finale in Zermatt, I booked a mountaineering trek to the summit of Breithorn. My AI's decision to use a guide was validated five days earlier, at the top station, when I met two young would-be summiteers who fell 30 feet into a crevasse shortly after stepping onto the glacier. After a couple of false starts, conditions finally cleared enough for us to go. We snaked across the glacier like a prison chain-gang, tethered to one another, awkward in our snowshoes. Our guide in front would occasionally stop to test the ground before deeming it safe enough to cross. As the slope steepened, we swapped out our snowshoes for crampons. The spikes bit into the snow, letting us walk straight up the steepest of it. Our guide would yell "konzentrieren!" and "no faffing about here!" whenever we got to a treacherous stretch. All out of breath and in desperate need of a break, we finally summited, taking in the expansive view of the Alps around us.

Breithorn is 13,661 feet. Larkya La is still three thousand feet higher.
